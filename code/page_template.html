Coast or herds? A Monte Carlo test of the route out of Africa

A paleo-landscape model of 65,000–85,000 years ago, run __N__ × 2 times. It compares a coast-hugging, shellfish-eating dispersal with one that follows big game inland.

Short answer
  • Your instinct holds up on speed. In paired runs (same climate, same sea level, same walking speed), the coastal strategy reached Sahul first in of runs, by a median of about years.
  • Your "the sites are sunk" point is real and measurable. A median of the coastal route is now underwater, vs for the inland route, and this grid undercounts it.
  • Pure beachcombing loses on fit to the evidence. Humans were in interior Arabia (the Nefud lakes, ~95–55 ka), and a coast-only population rarely gets there. The best-fitting model is a mixed forager: coast-anchored, but pushing up rivers and into green inland basins when rain allowed.
  • The current evidence can't break the tie. Fitting the model to the known sites pins down speed (~3 km/yr) and an early exit (~80 ka, in a wet phase), but not how rich the coast was. Drowned sites, mostly in 10–50 m of water, are the test.
  • The deciding assumption is coastal productivity. If the shore was at least ~85% as rich as the best savanna, the coastal route wins almost every time. Below that, it doesn't.

First, a correction: the seas were lower, not higher

During the exit window, ice sheets held so much water that sea level sat roughly 60–90 m below today's. The Persian Gulf was a dry river valley, the "Gulf Oasis", fed by the Tigris-Euphrates and springs. Bab-el-Mandeb narrowed to a strait a few kilometres wide, and the Sunda shelf joined Sumatra, Java and Borneo to Asia. The coastline people would have followed lies 50–150 km offshore of today's, under water. That makes your hypothesis hard to test from sites, which is exactly why it's worth modelling.

How the model works

It is a time-dependent least-cost dispersal model. The map is a grid of ~79,000 cells from Libya to New Guinea. Every 1,000 years, sea level redraws the coast, and a humidity index switches the Saharo-Arabian desert belt between "Green Arabia" savanna and hyper-arid desert. Each strategy turns that landscape into a speed surface: how fast a foraging population's frontier can advance through each cell. A frontier can't wait in a cell that turns uninhabitable, so a population caught inland when the rains fail dies out. Sea gaps cost an expected waiting time that grows steeply with width. Both strategies get the same boats.

StrategyWhat makes a cell good
CoastalWithin ~37 km of the shore. Quality = coastal productivity × fresh water. Arid coasts keep some water from coastal springs even in dry phases. Inland is used only weakly. No re-learning cost while staying on the shore.
MegafaunaGrassland and game. Quality = biome × rainfall pulse × altitude, with river corridors (Nile, Tigris-Euphrates, Indus, the Gulf basin) as refuges. Pays a re-tooling delay every time it enters a new biome.

Climate inputs (median and 10–90% range across runs)

Every uncertain number is drawn at random in each run, from wide ranges. That covers sea level (±8 m, ±1.5 kyr), the timing and strength of each humid pulse, monsoon strength, front speed (0.7–6 km/yr), coastal productivity (0.7–1.2× the best savanna), coastal fresh water in droughts, re-tooling cost (0–1,500 yr), and sea-crossing ability. All three strategies are run on the same draw, so the comparisons are paired.

The megafauna route crosses Arabia during the wet MIS 5a window, then fans across Iran, north India and the dry Sunda savanna.

What actually decides it

Head-to-head, coastal beats megafauna in of paired runs. That margin almost entirely comes down to one assumption: how productive the shore was compared with good savanna.

When the shore is rich, coastal fresh water persists through droughts, and moving between biomes is costly, coastal wins in of runs. When the shore is poorer and biome changes are cheap, it wins only . Your "humans are lazy" intuition maps onto exactly this. The beach is one predictable, low-effort habitat stretching from Eritrea to Timor. The only questions are whether it really was as rich as the savanna, and whether there was drinking water on the Arabian and Makran coasts in dry phases.

Experiment: what does the evidence actually tell us?

I kept only the runs whose outcome matches all three long-chronology facts: Sulawesi by 67.8 ka, Sumatra by 63 ka, and interior Arabia occupied during the wet phase. Then I looked at what those surviving runs have in common. This is rejection-style approximate Bayesian inference.

…and by exit date

Three findings. (1) The evidence needs fast movement: below ~1.5 km/yr almost nothing gets to Sulawesi in time, and the surviving runs centre on ~3 km/yr. (2) It needs an early exit, during Green Arabia (80–85 ka works far better than 72–75 ka). (3) It says nothing about coastal productivity, the one assumption that decides coast vs herds. That parameter's distribution in the surviving runs is unchanged from what went in (median both before and after). The current dry-land record can't settle your question. Only drowned sites can. Among the runs that do fit the evidence, the coastal group still reaches Sahul before the megafauna group of the time.

Half of the drowned route lies in under ~ m of water, which is diver and sub-bottom-sonar territory. The Bay of Bengal–Myanmar shelf, the Sunda shelf and the Gujarat–Indus shelf hold most of it. The Red Sea and south Arabian shelves are narrow enough that this 37 km grid misses them, so treat their small shares as an artefact, not a signal.

Verdict

The model supports a softened version of your view. A coastal track is the fastest and most climate-proof way to get from the Horn to Sahul, and it predicts the sparse record we actually see, because roughly half of it is now under 60–90 m of water. But "simply following the coast" is less likely than people using the coast as a highway while making inland excursions when rains opened up the interior. Megafauna hunting alone fits the interior Arabian sites but crosses the Arabian and Iranian deserts only in brief windows. The mixed strategy is the most likely in both chronologies.

What would move the needle: dated sites on the drowned shelves the model flags (Gujarat–Indus, Bay of Bengal–Myanmar, Sunda, plus the narrow Red Sea and south Arabian shelves this grid can't resolve), shell middens older than 60 ka between Yemen and India, and better estimates of how fast coastal foragers actually expand.

Caveats

This is a deliberately simple model. Biomes are hand-drawn boxes, softened at the edges, not a paleoclimate simulation. Humidity is a single index for the whole desert belt. Sea-level curve, humidity pulse dates and coastal productivity are approximations from the literature, sampled with wide uncertainty. The 20′ grid overstates strait widths, so the model subtracts ~25 km per crossing to compensate. There is no population size, Toba eruption, competition with Neanderthals or Denisovans, or multiple waves. Results describe the logic of the strategies, not a date for any event.
