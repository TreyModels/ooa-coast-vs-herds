Coast or herds? A Monte Carlo test of the route out of Africa

A paleo-landscape model of 65,000–85,000 years ago, run __N__ × 2 times. It compares a coast-hugging, shellfish-eating dispersal with one that follows big game inland.

Short answer
  • Your instinct holds up on speed. In paired runs (same climate, same sea level, same walking speed), the coastal strategy reached Sahul first in of runs, by a median of about years.
  • Your "the sites are sunk" point is real and measurable. A median of the coastal route is now underwater, vs for the inland route, and this grid undercounts it.
  • Pure beachcombing loses on fit to the evidence. Humans were in interior Arabia (the Nefud lakes, ~95–55 ka), and a coast-only population rarely gets there. The best-fitting model is a mixed forager: coast-anchored, but pushing up rivers and into green inland basins when rain allowed.
  • The current evidence can't break the tie. Fitting the model to the known sites pins down speed (~3 km/yr) and an early exit (~80 ka, in a wet phase), but not how rich the coast was. Drowned sites, mostly in 10–50 m of water, are the test.
  • The DNA side fits a coast-anchored spread. Papuans branch as sisters to East Asians in 94–100% of coastal and mixed runs, against ~55% for the megafauna strategy. Any earlier wave, such as whoever made the 67,800-year-old Sulawesi art, must have nearly vanished, or Papuans would carry far more than the ≤2% of its DNA genomes allow.
  • Homo erectus wasn't a beachcomber, and didn't paddle. Its oldest sites are inland and northern, which coast-bound runs usually miss. Its islands (Sulawesi, Flores, Luzon) need sea crossings, but canoes would have put it in Australia too. Rare accidental rafting fits best.
  • The deciding assumption is coastal productivity. If the shore was at least ~85% as rich as the best savanna, the coastal route wins almost every time. Below that, it doesn't.

First, a correction: the seas were lower, not higher

During the exit window, ice sheets held so much water that sea level sat roughly 60–90 m below today's. The Persian Gulf was a dry river valley, the "Gulf Oasis", fed by the Tigris-Euphrates and springs. Bab-el-Mandeb narrowed to a strait a few kilometres wide, and the Sunda shelf joined Sumatra, Java and Borneo to Asia. The coastline people would have followed lies 50–150 km offshore of today's, under water. That makes your hypothesis hard to test from sites, which is exactly why it's worth modelling.

How the model works

It is a time-dependent least-cost dispersal model. The map is a grid of ~79,000 cells from Libya to New Guinea. Every 1,000 years, sea level redraws the coast, and a humidity index switches the Saharo-Arabian desert belt between "Green Arabia" savanna and hyper-arid desert. Each strategy turns that landscape into a speed surface: how fast a foraging population's frontier can advance through each cell. A frontier can't wait in a cell that turns uninhabitable, so a population caught inland when the rains fail dies out. Sea gaps cost an expected waiting time that grows steeply with width. Both strategies get the same boats.

StrategyWhat makes a cell good
CoastalWithin ~37 km of the shore. Quality = coastal productivity × fresh water. Arid coasts keep some water from coastal springs even in dry phases. Inland is used only weakly. No re-learning cost while staying on the shore.
MegafaunaGrassland and game. Quality = biome × rainfall pulse × altitude, with river corridors (Nile, Tigris-Euphrates, Indus, the Gulf basin) as refuges. Pays a re-tooling delay every time it enters a new biome.
MixedTakes whichever is better in each cell.

Climate inputs (median and 10–90% range across runs)

Every uncertain number is drawn at random in each run, from wide ranges. That covers sea level (±8 m, ±1.5 kyr), the timing and strength of each humid pulse, monsoon strength, front speed (0.7–6 km/yr), coastal productivity (0.7–1.2× the best savanna), coastal fresh water in droughts, re-tooling cost (0–1,500 yr), and sea-crossing ability. All three strategies are run on the same draw, so the comparisons are paired.

The megafauna route crosses Arabia during the wet MIS 5a window, then fans across Iran, north India and the dry Sunda savanna.

What actually decides it

Head-to-head, coastal beats megafauna in of paired runs. That margin almost entirely comes down to one assumption: how productive the shore was compared with good savanna.

Coastal wins (%) by cost of re-learning each new biome

When the shore is rich, coastal fresh water persists through droughts, and moving between biomes is costly, coastal wins in of runs. When the shore is poorer and biome changes are cheap, it wins only . Your "humans are lazy" intuition maps onto exactly this. The beach is one predictable, low-effort habitat stretching from Eritrea to Timor. The only questions are whether it really was as rich as the savanna, and whether there was drinking water on the Arabian and Makran coasts in dry phases.

Experiment: what does the evidence actually tell us?

I kept only the runs whose outcome matches all three long-chronology facts: Sulawesi by 67.8 ka, Sumatra by 63 ka, and interior Arabia occupied during the wet phase. Then I looked at what those surviving runs have in common. This is rejection-style approximate Bayesian inference.

Which strategy the evidence favours

Share of runs matching the evidence, by front speed

…and by exit date

Three findings. (1) The evidence needs fast movement: below ~1.5 km/yr almost nothing gets to Sulawesi in time, and the surviving runs centre on ~3 km/yr. (2) It needs an early exit, during Green Arabia (80–85 ka works far better than 72–75 ka). (3) It says nothing about coastal productivity, the one assumption that decides coast vs herds. That parameter's distribution in the surviving runs is unchanged from what went in (median both before and after). The current dry-land record can't settle your question. Only drowned sites can. Among the runs that do fit the evidence, the coastal group still reaches Sahul before the megafauna group of the time.

Experiment: where to look underwater

Taking the coastal and mixed routes, which cells did people pass through that are seabed today, and how deep?

RegionShare of drowned routeTypical depth

Half of the drowned route lies in under ~ m of water, which is diver and sub-bottom-sonar territory. The Bay of Bengal–Myanmar shelf, the Sunda shelf and the Gujarat–Indus shelf hold most of it. The Red Sea and south Arabian shelves are narrow enough that this 37 km grid misses them, so treat their small shares as an artefact, not a signal.

Experiment: does a modelled paleoclimate change the answer?

I swapped the hand-drawn habitat for one taken from a climate simulation. Per-cell net primary production, desert fraction and temperature come from the LOVECLIM transient run behind Timmermann & Friedrich (2016), regridded to the 20′ grid. The climate sets both big-game habitat and the biome classes that cost re-tooling time. Sea level, coastlines, rivers, crossings and the solver are unchanged. Runs are paired: every draw runs coastal, megafauna and mixed on both climates with identical parameters and noise. The hand-drawn runs still reproduce the published output (largest relative difference 4e-5).

Three findings. (1) The coastal advantage grows and depends less on the shore. Coastal now wins 71% of runs even when the coast is poorest. (2) Exit timing and the Arabian desert decide it. LOVECLIM's Arabia is under 15% desert at 82–84 ka and about 80% desert at 66–75 ka, so herds do well only when they leave early: coastal wins 57% of the earliest-exit runs and 99% of the latest. (3) The evidence still cannot pick a winner. Mixed fits best only when flexibility is free; at a 20% speed cost it ties megafauna. Coastal fits worst only because of the Nefud test. Without it, coastal fits better than megafauna.

Caveats. This is one coarse climate simulation, not an ensemble. The productivity-to-habitat mapping is assumed. Small Lesser Sunda islands that LOVECLIM leaves barren are refilled from nearby land. An earlier variant (13 November) kept the hand-drawn biome classes and gave 92% coastal wins; the numbers above use the climate for both. Full numbers and code: results/loveclim/README.md.

What it means for the DNA we see today

Next I put a genetics layer on the dispersal runs. The ancestors of everyone living outside Africa spread from a hub on the Persian Plateau after the shared Neanderthal mixing (start 50–46 ka, ending 25 ka). I ran each strategy 150 times and traced who founded whom. That tree gives three predictions that genomes can check: branching order, diversity loss along the route, and how much DNA would be picked up from an earlier wave already living there.

1 · Branching order

Predicted treeCoastalMegafaunaMixed

Genomes put Papuans and Australians inside the East Eurasian branch, as a clade with East Asians, after a first east/west split. South Indian hunter-gatherer ancestry, the Andamanese, Papuans and East Asians all branch off within a short span. Every strategy gets the east/west split first (median ka vs ka for the eastern splits). Every strategy also produces a near-star: the eastern branches all split within a median thousand years of each other. But only the coast-anchored strategies reliably make Papuans and East Asians sisters, because both descend from the same Southeast Asian shoreline population. Moving overland, East Asia is just as often reached from India via the north.

2 · Diversity loss along the route

CoastalMegafaunaMixed

Heterozygosity relative to the hub. Each founding step loses diversity in proportion to how narrow the advancing front was. All three strategies reproduce the observed pattern, with diversity falling from west to east and lowest in New Guinea and Australia. A coastline is a one-dimensional corridor, so the coastal front is thin and drift bites roughly twice as hard. That shows up as a steeper gradient, but its absolute size depends on group sizes I can't pin down, so on its own this can't separate the strategies. The scale is set so mixed-strategy Papuans lose 15%.

3 · The ghost of an earlier wave

Suppose the people behind the 67,800-year-old Sulawesi art, and any other pre-60 ka groups, were modern humans from an earlier wave still living along the route. The later wave would then walk through their land all the way to New Guinea. In the median run, Papuan ancestors cross of earlier-wave territory. Each stretch they pass through mixes in a little local DNA, and that adds up.

Later wave coastalMegafaunaMixed┈ 2% (highest published estimate)

Genomes allow at most about 2% of Papuan DNA from an earlier wave (Pagani et al. 2016), and other teams found none. Staying under 2% requires less than about 1 interbreeding in per 37-km stretch of shared land. Modern humans clearly mixed with Neanderthals, so neighbouring groups of their own species almost certainly mixed more than that. So the early wave must have been gone or nearly gone by ~50 ka along the southern route, or it wasn't made of our ancestors' kind of human. One suggestion is that the earliest Sahul and Wallacea signs could be Denisovans. This holds whichever strategy each wave used.

What ancient DNA would need to show if your coastal model is right: (1) Genomes from drowned or coastal sites between India and Sunda, if any survive, should sit on the Papuan–East Asian branch, with drift increasing step by step along the coastline. (2) Inland Paleolithic lineages, such as the ghost lineage in Tibet or Tianyuan in north China, should turn out to be later offshoots of coastal populations, not older parallel branches. (3) Any Sahul or Sulawesi genome older than 50 ka would either be a dead-end lineage or not Homo sapiens. Tropical heat destroys DNA, so such genomes may never be recovered, but (1) and (2) are testable.

Homo erectus: the same test 2 million years earlier

I ran the same engine for the first humans to leave Africa: start 2.6–2.25 million years ago, run to 100,000 years ago, 400 draws. Each draw uses three foraging strategies and three sea-crossing abilities: walking only, rare accidental rafting (floating vegetation, storm or tsunami debris, once every tens to hundreds of thousands of years) and dugout canoes (deliberate crossings, same ability as the sapiens runs). Sea level follows two histories, 50/50: the classic one with shallower early ice ages, and Clark et al. 2025, where early lowstands were as deep as the last ice age. That choice barely changed anything. Each version is scored against ten places erectus-grade humans are found by a given date, and three places they never reached before modern humans.

Coast or inland? Mixed strategy, rafting

TestCoastalMegafaunaMixed

For erectus, a coast-bound population usually arrives too late at the oldest sites, Shangchen on the Loess Plateau (2.1 Ma) and Dmanisi in the Caucasus (1.85 Ma), which are inland and far north. Inland and mixed foragers hit them every time. The earliest humans out of Africa weren't beachcombers. That makes the coastal habit of the sapiens runs look like something new, not the default.

Boats or not? Mixed strategy

TestWalk onlyRare raftingDugout canoes

Walking alone can't explain the record. Sulawesi (≥1.04 Ma), Flores (1.02 Ma) and Luzon (0.71 Ma) are never reached, so something crossed water. Dugout canoes overshoot. They reach the islands, but in of runs they also reach New Guinea or Australia, usually within a few thousand years of leaving Africa. Neither has any trace of humans before sapiens. Canoes reach the Andamans in of runs, which are also empty of earlier humans. Only rare, accidental crossings fit both sides: the islands that were reached, and Sahul, which wasn't.

Runs that fit everything, by how often a lucky crossing happens

The sweet spot is roughly one successful rafting event per thousand years at a short gap. That's often enough to hit Wallacea and the Philippines over a million years, and rare enough never to make the 90 km jump from Timor to Australia. The wide water of Wallace's Line worked as a filter, not a wall.

Japan is left out of the score. At glacial lowstands the model lets erectus walk in via Korea, and no accepted pre-modern site exists there. Either that land bridge never fully opened, which is debated, or the record is missing. As with the sapiens runs, these are hand-drawn biomes and approximate dates. Read it for logic, not precise ages.

Verdict

The model supports a softened version of your view. A coastal track is the fastest and most climate-proof way to get from the Horn to Sahul, and it predicts the sparse record we actually see, because roughly half of it is now under 60–90 m of water. But "simply following the coast" is less likely than people using the coast as a highway while making inland excursions when rains opened up the interior. Megafauna hunting alone fits the interior Arabian sites but crosses the Arabian and Iranian deserts only in brief windows. The mixed strategy leads only when switching is free: it reaches Sahul first in 79% of runs at zero switching cost but 40% at a 20% speed cost. Its fit lead (posterior 0.44 against 0.33 megafauna and 0.23 coastal) comes mostly from the Nefud constraint; without it the three are close (0.39, 0.30, 0.31). Under the modelled LOVECLIM climate, coastal reaches Sahul before megafauna in 82.5% of runs, and without the Nefud test it fits better than megafauna. See REVIEW_RESPONSE.md and results/loveclim/README.md.

What would move the needle: dated sites on the drowned shelves the model flags (Gujarat–Indus, Bay of Bengal–Myanmar, Sunda, plus the narrow Red Sea and south Arabian shelves this grid can't resolve), shell middens older than 60 ka between Yemen and India, and better estimates of how fast coastal foragers actually expand.

Caveats

This is a deliberately simple model. Biomes are hand-drawn boxes, softened at the edges, not a paleoclimate simulation (the LOVECLIM experiment swaps in a modelled one). Humidity is a single index for the whole desert belt. Sea-level curve, humidity pulse dates and coastal productivity are approximations from the literature, sampled with wide uncertainty. The 20′ grid overstates strait widths, so the model subtracts ~25 km per crossing to compensate. There is no population size, Toba eruption, competition with Neanderthals or Denisovans, or multiple waves. Results describe the logic of the strategies, not a date for any event.
